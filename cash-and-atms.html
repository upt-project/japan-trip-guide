Cash and ATMs in Japan: which ATMs take foreign cards

The first ATM most visitors try - the one in a Japanese bank lobby - usually rejects a foreign card. The machines that work are in convenience stores and post offices. Here is which ones, what they charge, and how much cash you still need.

Do you still need cash in Japan?

Yes, some. Japan's cashless payment ratio reached 58% in 2025 (Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry), which means over 40% of payments are still cash. Cards and phone payments cover hotels, department stores and most chain restaurants, but cash is still the only option at many small restaurants, at shrine and temple admission desks (typically ¥300-¥500), for charging an IC card at older machines, and in rural areas. A sensible pattern: withdraw a working amount, keep ¥10,000 or so in reserve, and top up at convenience stores as you go.

Your own bank may add its own overseas fee on top of the ATM's fee - that part is set at home, not in Japan.

The on-screen choice that costs you money

Some ATMs offer to bill you in your home currency instead of yen (dynamic currency conversion). The rate is almost always worse than your bank's. Choose yen, and let your own bank do the conversion.

A simple cash routine

  • Land, withdraw once at a Seven Bank ATM at the airport, and you are set for the first days.
  • Use an IC card (Suica, Pasmo, Icoca) for trains and convenience stores - it shrinks the coins you carry.
  • Keep small notes and coins for shrines, market stalls and older ticket machines.
  • Before a rural side trip, top up in the city: foreign-card ATMs thin out quickly outside urban areas.
