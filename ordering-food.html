Ordering food in Japan: menus, ticket machines and useful phrases

The menu is in Japanese, the staff speak Japanese, and you are hungry. Ordering still works, because most Japanese restaurants are built around systems that do not need a conversation: a machine, a tablet, a photo menu, or a counter where you point. Learn which system you are in first, then order inside it.

The four ways to order

SystemHow it worksWhere you see it
Ticket machineBuy a ticket at the machine by the door, hand it to staff when you sit downRamen shops, udon and soba counters, fast lunch spots
Tablet or QR at the tableOrder on the tablet or your phone; the food arrives, the bill stays on the screenChain restaurants, conveyor-belt sushi, family restaurants
Menu, call the staffPoint at the menu and say "kore kudasai" (this one, please); staff come when you press the call button or say "sumimasen"Most sit-down restaurants, cafes
Order and pay at the registerOrder at the counter, pay there, then take a seatMarket stalls and food halls, e.g. Kuromon Sanpei in Osaka: order and pay at the register before being seated

Ticket machines usually have an English button and take cash; some take IC cards. If the machine has no English, the buttons are almost always in the same order as the printed menu on the wall, with photos. Take your time: nobody behind you minds a first-timer reading the machine, and staff will help if you point and look lost.

Paying: at the table or at the register?

  • Pay at the register on the way out is the default in most restaurants. Take the bill from the table to the register; tipping is not done in Japan.
  • Pay at the table happens in some izakaya and higher-end places, where the bill is brought to you.
  • Cash-only counters are common and easy to miss: Daikokuya Tempura in Asakusa is cash only — no credit cards, e-money or QR payments — and Kushikatsu Daruma in Osaka is cash only too. Carry cash for lunch every day; see which ATMs take foreign cards.
  • Set meals (teishoku, 定食) are the safe default: a main dish, rice, miso soup and pickles for one price, usually ¥1,000–¥2,000 at lunch. Point at the set meal and you have ordered a complete lunch.

When the menu is only in Japanese

Three tools, in order of reliability: the photo menu or the plastic food models in the window (point at them); your phone camera with a translation app (good for the menu, useless for the conversation); and the phrases below. If a dish name contains a word you recognise — ramen, udon, soba, donburi (rice bowl), teishoku (set meal) — you already know most of what you are ordering.

Phrases that actually get used

Show the Japanese, or say the romaji. Staff hear these every day; perfect pronunciation is not required.

Allergies: show it, do not just say it. Saying "peanuts" out loud can be misheard, and a busy kitchen cannot guess how serious it is. Write the allergy on your phone — for example, 「ピーナッツアレルギーがあります。ピーナッツが入っている料理は食べられません。」 ("I have a peanut allergy. I cannot eat dishes containing peanuts.") — and show the screen to staff before you order. If they hesitate, that is your answer: they are telling you they cannot guarantee it, and you should eat elsewhere. Allergy cards you can hand over already exist on Etsy; a phone note does the same job for free.

At an izakaya: the bill is part of ordering

In an izakaya (Japanese pub), a small dish called otoshi is put in front of you as soon as you sit down, before anything you ordered arrives — usually ¥300–¥500 per person. It is a seat charge, not a dish you ordered, and you cannot refuse it once you are seated: sitting down means you have accepted it. The moment that matters is before you sit down — check the menu by the door for お通し and ask "Otoshi wa ikura desu ka?" (how much is the otoshi?). If you do not like the answer, that is when you can still choose another shop. The full breakdown — otoshi, seating charges and service charges, with a bill checklist — is in the Japan Izakaya Bill Decoder ($4.99).

If the bill looks wrong

Ask at the register first, politely, pointing at the line. Most confusion ends there. A normal otoshi is never a reason to escalate. If you were charged far beyond the normal range with no explanation and it cannot be resolved, Japan has an official Consumer Hotline for Tourists, run by the National Consumer Affairs Center of Japan: 03-5449-0906, weekdays 10:00–12:00 and 13:00–16:00, in English and six other languages. It gives advice; it cannot order a refund.
