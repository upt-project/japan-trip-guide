Navigating Japanese train stations: transfers, gates and exits

The map makes it look like one network. It is not. Tokyo has JR East, Tokyo Metro, Toei Subway and private railways sharing the same stations, each with its own gates and fares. Once you know that one fact, the rest is just reading signs.

Start with an IC card

One IC card (Suica, PASMO, ICOCA and the rest) taps through JR, Metro, Toei, private railways and most buses, and works in convenience stores too. Tap in where you start, tap out where you finish, and the fare is deducted automatically. A Welcome Suica for short-term visitors is sold at Narita and Haneda airports and at JR East Travel Service Centers: no deposit, preloaded ¥1,000–¥10,000, valid 28 days including the purchase day, and the remaining balance is not refundable. One card per person — children need their own child card at half fare. JR East does not accept contactless credit cards at its gates, so an IC card is still needed for the Yamanote Line. Paper tickets cost more than IC fares on JR since the March 2026 fare rise, so pay per ride with an IC card unless a day pass clearly covers your route.

The three kinds of transfer

Changing trains is where people get confused, because the gate decides what kind of transfer it is:

TransferWhat happensFare
Same company, same stationWalk to the other platform, no gate in betweenOne fare for the whole trip
Different companiesTap out of one company's gates, tap into the other'sTwo separate fares — JR to Metro or Toei is charged as two trips
Metro to Toei (or the reverse)Use a designated transfer gate, within 60 minutes¥70 transfer discount, applied automatically

Some through-services run straight from one company's tracks onto another's, so you stay on the same train while the operator changes underneath you. Your IC card is charged for each company's section automatically. Do not try to calculate this by hand: a route-planning app (Google Maps or a Japan transit app) shows the fare for each route, and your IC card handles the charging.

The mistake to avoid: buying a day pass that stops halfway. The Tokyo Subway Ticket (24 hours, ¥1,000) covers Metro and Toei only — not JR, not buses. On the Tokyo one-day route it covers only ¥565 of rides. The Kyoto Subway & Bus 1-Day Pass (¥1,100) covers only ¥460 of bus rides on the Kyoto route, and the old ¥700 bus-only pass was discontinued in September 2023. Check what your pass actually covers before you buy it.

Reading the signs: line, number, direction

  • Lines are colour-coded and numbered. Metro station codes use a letter tied to the line (G for Ginza, M for Marunouchi) plus a station number. Follow the colour and the number, not just the station name.
  • Direction is given by the terminus station. A platform sign says where the train ends, not where you are going. Match the terminus on the sign to the direction your route needs.
  • There are four separate Asakusa stations in Tokyo, and the Tsukuba Express one is about 10 minutes' walk from the others. At Shibuya, use the Hachiko Exit for the crossing and the statue; at Shinjuku, use the west exit for the Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building. Following a map pin to the wrong station or exit can add 15–20 minutes of walking with luggage.
  • Platform numbers are per operator. JR Kyoto Station's Nara Line leaves from platforms 8–10; the same station's other lines have their own platforms elsewhere in the building. The platform number only makes sense with the operator and line attached.

Exits: the number matters more than the name

Big stations have dozens of exits, and the wrong one can put you on the wrong side of a huge building. Decide your exit before you arrive: the route apps show it, and the guides on this site name it (Kuromon Market is 5 minutes from Nippombashi Exit 10; the Umeda Sky Building is about 9 minutes from Umeda North Gate). Inside the station, follow the exit number on the overhead signs rather than the street name. If you come out at the wrong exit, do not re-enter and pay again — walk around the building at street level first; most exits are a few minutes apart on foot.

How big is big?

Shinjuku Station is the world's busiest railway station by Guinness World Records, with about 3.5 million passengers a day across JR East, Keio, Odakyu, Tokyo Metro and Toei — roughly the population of Yokohama passing through one station every day. Tokyo Station, Shibuya, Ikebukuro and Osaka-Umeda are the same kind of place: a small city underground, with shops, restaurants and passageways that look like streets. Two rules for these stations:

  • Allow 10–15 minutes for a transfer at a big station, more with luggage. The walk between a JR platform and a subway platform can be longer than the train ride you just took.
  • Meet people outside the gates, at a named exit — "Hachiko Exit" works; "Shibuya Station" does not.

If something goes wrong

  • Wrong train: get off at the next station and cross to the opposite platform. You have not lost your fare — the IC card charges on exit, by distance actually travelled.
  • Not enough balance: use the fare adjustment machine (精算機) beside the gates before you exit. It tops up the difference; do not jump the gate.
  • Wrong gate, paper ticket: at some Metro stations a transfer means stepping outside the gates. Use the orange transfer gates so your ticket keeps working, and do not take too long — there is a time limit.
  • Last train: city trains stop around midnight. If you miss it, the choice is a taxi or waiting for the first train at about 5:00. Check the last departure before a late dinner, not after it.
